The Consequences of Gentrification on Incumbent Residents (Job Market Paper)
Amine Derdari

Cities in post-industrial countries have drawn college graduates back into neighbourhoods that were abandoned a generation earlier. I estimate how this inflow of highly qualified residents into low-educated neighbourhoods—what I define as gentrification— affects incumbent children in 108 English cities between 2001 and 2011. Most evidence on neighbourhood effects comes from children who move; I focus instead on those who stay while their neighbourhood changes around them. Because neighbourhoods that attract educated in-movers already differ in ways that matter for children, I instrument this change with the stock of buildings listed before 2001. In gentrifying neighbourhoods, incumbent children are 7.8 percentage points more likely to make expected progress in mathematics and 6.2 points more likely in English, while OLS estimates are close to zero. Gains concentrate along ethnic and linguistic lines rather than economic ones: White and English-native pupils gain most, while I cannot reject that disadvantaged pupils gain as much as their peers. Across neighbourhoods, gains are largest where residents were predominantly White and private renting was rare, and show no detectable difference by baseline deprivation. Work in progress on mechanisms suggests that the effect operates in the neighbourhood rather than through schools or household resources.

Understanding Gentrification through Urban Connectivity: A Machine Learning Approach
Amine Derdari and Alessandro Venerandi

This project uses random forest models to explore the relationship between gentrification and measures of urban street-network connectivity, including straightness, betweenness, and localness. The goal is to uncover which dimensions of neighbourhood connectivity are most predictive of gentrification dynamics.
